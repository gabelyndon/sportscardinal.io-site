There's a difference between the players everyone talks about and the players whose cards are actually changing hands. The gap is bigger than you'd think.

GemRate tracks individual card sales on eBay and publishes monthly rankings. The numbers tell a story that runs against a lot of hobby conventional wisdom.

> **Figures below are July 2026 eBay sales via GemRate.** Monthly data typically posts a few weeks after month end, so check for the latest before acting on any of this.

## The top of the board

**Michael Jordan — $19.39M, up 16%**

The best-selling athlete in the hobby last played an NBA game in 2003. His cards did $68.89 million in the first half of 2026 alone, and July was his strongest month of the run.

Four Jordan cards cleared $60,000 in July, topped by a 2005 Upper Deck Exquisite Enshrinements auto numbered to 25 at $65,000. Three of the four were Star Company cards from his 1984-85 rookie season.

**Shohei Ohtani — $16.48M, down 2%**

The best-selling athlete over the first half of the year at $85.67 million, up 125% from the prior six months. July was essentially flat, which for a market this size is a non-event.

**LeBron James — $8.54M, up 51%**

A 51% jump in a month is enormous at that dollar level.

**Kobe Bryant — $6.04M, up 7%**
**Tom Brady — $5.93M, up 24%**
**Lionel Messi — $5.89M, up 47%**

Look at that list again. Of the top six, four are retired and one of them died in 2020.

## The other side

The same month, the young players the hobby chased all spring got hit hard.

- **Victor Wembanyama:** $5.31M, down 62% — a drop of roughly $8.66 million from June
- **Jalen Brunson:** $940K, down 83%
- **Stephon Castle:** $310K, down 67%
- **Dylan Harper:** $1.76M, down 54%
- **Cooper Flagg:** $4M, down 16%

Brunson is the cleanest illustration of the whole dynamic. His cards jumped 296% in June on the back of a Finals run, hitting $5.32 million and putting him sixth among all athletes. One month later, once the season ended, he was down 83%.

Nothing about Brunson changed. The games stopped, and that was enough.

## What this actually tells you

**Legacy money is steadier than hype money.** Jordan's market doesn't need him to do anything. There's no season, no injury risk, no bad stretch that tanks the value. The collector base is people buying a fixed, finished legacy. That's a fundamentally different demand curve than a rookie whose value depends on next Tuesday's box score.

**Active players trade on the calendar.** If you're buying current NBA or NFL players, you're buying into a seasonal cycle whether you meant to or not. Prices inflate during playoff runs and deflate the moment the season ends. The Brunson chart is that cycle in its purest form.

If you have discipline, that's an opportunity rather than a warning. The time to buy an active player is the dead middle of their offseason, when nobody's paying attention. The time to sell is during the run everyone's watching. That's easy to say and hard to do, because the offseason is exactly when a player feels least exciting.

**Rookie prices carry a hype premium that decays.** Flagg, Harper, and Castle were all down in July. That doesn't mean they're bad players or bad long-term holds. It means the price you pay in the middle of rookie-season excitement includes a premium for attention, and attention doesn't last.

**Volume isn't value.** These are total dollars moved, not price per card. Jordan's number reflects both expensive cards and a lot of cards. High volume means liquidity — you can sell when you want to — which is worth something on its own.

## How I'd use this

If you're collecting for enjoyment, none of this should change what you buy. Collect who you like.

If you're paying attention to value, the takeaway is that the market rewards patience over reaction. The names at the top of the volume list got there by being permanently, boringly desirable. The names at the bottom got there by being briefly exciting.

That's not a knock on the young guys. It's a note about timing. Buy the excitement after it fades, not while it's peaking.
