Most people play their first round of Guess The Price of the Card, finish with 380 points, and assume the scoring is harsh. It isn't. It's just measuring something different from what they think it's measuring.

Here's the whole system, and what to do with it.

## You're scored on ratios, not dollars

This is the part that trips everyone up. The game doesn't care how many dollars you missed by. It cares how many *times* off you were.

Say two cards come up. One is worth $30, the other $600. You guess $50 for the first and $620 for the second. You were off by $20 both times — but those are wildly different guesses. The first was 67% too high. The second was off by about three percent.

The game scores the second one near-perfect and the first one poorly, and it's right to. Anyone can get close on a $600 card by guessing "expensive." Getting close on a $30 card means you actually recognized what it was.

So stop thinking in dollars. Think in multiples.

## The curve

A perfect guess is 100 points. From there your score falls off smoothly as your guess drifts away from the real number — no cliffs, no brackets, no "you were in the right tier so here's partial credit." Every dollar closer earns you something.

The zero points are where it gets interesting:

- Guess **four times too high** and you score nothing.
- Guess **75% too low** — one quarter of the real price — and you score nothing.

Notice those aren't symmetrical in dollar terms, but they are in ratio terms. Guessing $400 on a $100 card and guessing $25 on a $100 card are the same size of mistake. The math treats them identically because they're both "off by a factor of four."

Ten cards per round. 100 points each. 1,000 possible.

## Lowballing is the more dangerous mistake

Here's the practical consequence, and it's the single most useful thing in this post.

Because you're being scored on multiples, the room you have on the high side is much wider in dollar terms than the room you have on the low side. On a $100 card, you can guess anywhere up to $400 before you hit zero — that's $300 of headroom. On the low side you hit zero at $25. That's $75 of room.

Most new players are afraid of looking foolish, so they guess low. It's the wrong instinct. If you're genuinely torn between $60 and $150 on a card, the $150 guess is the safer one.

## The streak threshold is more forgiving than you think

You keep a streak alive by scoring 50 or better on a card. In ratio terms, that means you can be roughly **72% too high** or **42% too low** and still stay alive.

Read that again, because it changes how you play. On a $200 card, anything from about $116 to $344 keeps your streak. That's an enormous window. You do not need to be sharp to hold a streak — you need to avoid being catastrophically wrong. Those are different skills.

Which means: when you have no idea, don't freeze and don't get cute. Make a defensible middle-of-the-road guess and move on. Streaks die from wild swings, not from being a little off.

## What the scores actually mean

- **Under 400.** You're guessing on vibes. Normal for a first round.
- **400–550.** You know the players but not the market. You're probably ignoring grade.
- **550–700.** Solid. You're reading condition and year correctly most of the time and getting burned on one or two outliers.
- **700–850.** You know what you're doing. At this level your losses are almost entirely on cards with unusual print runs or parallels.
- **850+.** Rare. This is someone who checks comps for fun.

Breaking 800 is the real benchmark. It requires eight or nine near-misses and no disasters, which is why the streak logic matters more than the perfect guesses.

## The three-step read

Whenever a card comes up, work through it in this order. The order matters, because each step multiplies the last.

**Start with the player.** Not "is he good" — is he *collected*. There are Hall of Famers whose cards are worth less than mid-tier players with passionate followings. Star power sets your baseline.

**Then adjust for grade, hard.** This is where most rounds are won and lost. The gap between a PSA 9 and a PSA 10 on the same card is routinely 3× or more, and on scarce vintage it can be an order of magnitude. A raw copy is a different animal again. If you take one habit away from this post, make it this: look at the grade before you look at anything else, and let it move your number aggressively.

**Then adjust for year and set.** Late-80s and early-90s cardboard was printed in volumes that are hard to comprehend now. A gorgeous, iconic, universally recognized card from 1990 can still be worth six dollars. Meanwhile a card from a short-printed insert set nobody remembers can be worth hundreds.

## One last thing

The scores aren't graded on a curve against other players, and they're not adjusted for difficulty. A 1,000 is genuinely available on every round. Nobody's getting it — but it's there.
